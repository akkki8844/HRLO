# Discussion

The results of this project support the central claim that reasoning is better understood as a **dynamic process** rather than a static outcome. Traditional assessments focus on whether a final answer is correct, but the PRLO system shows that correct answers can emerge from fundamentally different reasoning trajectories, some of which are unstable and fragile.

A key observation is that **controlled perturbations** to otherwise familiar problems produced measurable disruptions in reasoning behavior. These disruptions appeared as sudden increases in step-to-step latency, drops in latency entropy, and concurrent spikes in EMG activity. Importantly, these effects often occurred even when participants ultimately arrived at the correct solution. This suggests that correctness alone masks underlying cognitive strain and does not reliably reflect reasoning stability.

The identification of **collapse signatures** indicates that reasoning failure is not random. Across participants, similar perturbations produced comparable latency and EMG patterns, implying the existence of shared cognitive failure modes. This mirrors phenomena observed in engineered systems and machine learning models, where systems behave predictably under stress despite individual differences. The consistency of these signatures strengthens the argument that reasoning stability can be quantitatively characterized.

Using EMG as a physiological correlate of cognitive load proved to be a practical and effective design choice. Although EMG does not measure neural activity directly, involuntary muscle tension and micro-contractions are well-documented responses to increased cognitive effort and hesitation. By synchronizing EMG data with explicit reasoning steps, PRLO captures moments of internal struggle that are invisible to traditional testing methods. This low-cost approach also makes the system accessible and reproducible, an important consideration for educational and experimental research.

There are limitations to this study. The participant sample size was limited, and EMG signals are sensitive to movement artifacts and posture changes. While experimental controls were implemented to reduce noise, some variability is unavoidable. Additionally, the interpretation of EMG signals as indicators of cognitive load relies on indirect inference rather than direct measurement of brain activity. Future studies could integrate additional physiological signals, such as electrodermal activity or eye-tracking, to improve robustness.

Despite these limitations, the PRLO framework introduces a novel way to study reasoning by focusing on **stability, failure, and recovery** rather than performance alone. This perspective has potential applications in education, where identifying fragile understanding early could inform instructional design, as well as in human–computer interaction and AI-assisted learning. Overall, this work demonstrates that reasoning collapse is measurable, structured, and informative, offering a new lens for understanding how humans think under cognitive stress.
